From Sampling Distributions to Hypothesis Testing

Quick Review: Sampling Distributions

From Lab 6: the sampling distribution of the mean has

mean standard error
\(\mu_{\bar{X}} = \mu\) \(\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Learning check: samples of \(n\) = 36 from a population with \(\mu\) = 80, \(\sigma\) = 12

Two Distributions, Two z-Scores

Distribution of the data

○ Score: \(x\)

○ Mean: \(\mu\), SD: \(\sigma\)

○ \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\) (Lab 4!)

Sampling distribution

○ Score: \(\bar{x}\)

○ Mean: \(\mu\), SE: \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\)

○ \(z_{\bar{x}} = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}\)

The second one is our test statistic: how many standard errors is our sample mean away from the hypothesized population mean?

Seeing the Test Statistic

Population under \(H_0\): \(\mu\) = 10, \(\sigma\) = 2. We collect a sample of \(n\) = 20 with \(\bar{x}\) = 12

The curve is the sampling distribution if \(H_0\) were true; the blue line is where our sample lands

The z-Distribution in R

The norm() Family

A standard normal distribution is the z-distribution, so the norm() functions (Lab 4) do all the work

Function Gives you…
rnorm(n) n random z-values
pnorm(z) the probability at or below a z-value
qnorm(p) the z-value at a given probability

○ Default is mean = 0, sd = 1, so we don’t need to type them

pnorm(): From z to Probability

Note

pnorm() gives the area at or below the value. For a positive z, the p-value is the area above it, so use lower.tail = FALSE

These match the lecture: z = −1.00 → .16; z = 1.23 → .11

qnorm(): From Probability to z (Critical Values)

Where does ±1.96 come from?

○ Two-tailed: \(z_{crit}\) = ±1.96

○ One-tailed: \(z_{crit}\) = −1.65 (left) or +1.65 (right)

Getting a p-Value for Any z

-abs(z) always puts us in the lower tail, so this works for positive and negative z

Hypothesis Testing Step by Step

The Logic of Hypothesis Testing

1. State the hypotheses

○ \(H_0\): no effect / no difference (e.g., \(\mu\) = 100)

○ \(H_1\): there is an effect / a difference (e.g., \(\mu \neq\) 100)

2. Set the significance level: \(\alpha\) = .05, and find the critical value

3. Compute the test statistic (\(z\)) and its p-value, assuming \(H_0\) is true

4. Make a decision

Decision
\(p \le \alpha\), or \(|z| \ge |z_{crit}|\) Reject \(H_0\): statistically significant
\(p > \alpha\), or \(|z| < |z_{crit}|\) Fail to reject (retain) \(H_0\): not significant

ⓘ We never say we “accept” \(H_0\) or that \(H_0\) is true

Example: Weight of Cats

Normal cats: \(\mu\) = 100 g, \(\sigma\) = 40. A sample of \(n\) = 16 cats with prenatal alcohol exposure has \(\bar{x}\) = 70. Does alcohol affect birth weight?

\(z\) = −3 is far in the rejection region

\(p\) < .05 → reject \(H_0\)

Prenatal alcohol exposure has a statistically significant effect on cats’ birth weight

ⓘ The lecture reports the one-tail area, pnorm(-3) = .00135; the two-tailed p doubles it (.0027). Same decision either way

Your Turn: The “Evil” Professor

Your professor believes extra homework changes final exam scores. Population: \(\mu\) = 70, \(\sigma\) = 15. A sample of \(n\) = 25 students who got 50 extra HW questions a week scored \(\bar{x}\) = 75.

Check: \(z = \dfrac{75 - 70}{15 / \sqrt{25}} = \dfrac{5}{3}\) = 1.67, which is less than 1.96 → retain \(H_0\)

One-Tailed vs. Two-Tailed Tests

Directional vs. Non-Directional Hypotheses

Non-directional → two-tailed test

“There is a difference…”

○ \(H_1\): \(\mu \neq \mu_0\)

○ \(\alpha\)/2 = .025 in each tail

○ \(z_{crit}\) = ±1.96

Directional → one-tailed test

“…increases / decreases / better than…”

○ \(H_1\): \(\mu > \mu_0\) (upper) or \(\mu < \mu_0\) (lower)

○ all of \(\alpha\) = .05 in one tail

○ \(z_{crit}\) = +1.65 or −1.65

Practice: Directional or Non-Directional?

  1. There is a difference in body weights, with men weighing more than women.
  2. Women are better at concentrating on more than one thing at a time than men.
  3. There is a relationship between studying and final exam score.
  4. As employees earn more money, they become less likely to leave for a new job.

Directional: 1, 2, 4 | Non-directional: 3

The “Evil” Professor, Revisited

Now your professor believes extra homework increases final exam scores (same data: \(\mu\) = 70, \(\sigma\) = 15, \(n\) = 25, \(\bar{x}\) = 75)

Same data, same \(z\) = 1.67…

○ Two-tailed: 1.67 < 1.96 → retain \(H_0\)

○ Upper-tailed: 1.67 > 1.65 → reject \(H_0\)

This is why the direction must be decided before looking at the data!

Must-Take-Home Message

\[z_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}\]

○ pnorm() turns a z into a probability; use lower.tail = F or -abs(z) for the upper tail

○ qnorm() turns \(\alpha\) into a critical value: ±1.96 (two-tailed), ±1.65 (one-tailed)

○ Two-tailed p = 2 × one-tailed p

○ \(p \le \alpha\) → reject \(H_0\); \(p > \alpha\) → fail to reject (never “accept”) \(H_0\)

○ Directional hypothesis → one-tailed test; decide before collecting data
